MESH TYPES

Standard Diamond

45° diamond aperture — the most specified grade for hospitality and architectural screens. Balances open area with structural rigidity across the widest sheet sizes.

65–75% openHotel lobbies, facadesUp to 3,000mm

Hexagonal

Six-sided cells deliver higher open area with lower visual weight than diamond. The geometry reads as architectural detail at close range and dissolves to transparency at distance.

70–80% openRetail ceilings, facadesUp to 2,500mm

Micro \/ Fine

Tight 2–4mm cells create near-solid visual privacy while still passing air and diffuse light. Reads as a solid plane from normal viewing distance.

35–45% openPartitions, privacy screensUp to 1,800mm

Raised Flat

Diamond raised then cold-flattened gives a rigid, smooth-faced sheet with even finish. The structural grade for balustrades, stair infill panels, and load-bearing applications.

70–80% openBalustrades, stair infillUp to 3,000mm

How is a custom expanded metal panel quoted by material and finish for an architectural project?

Expanded metal panel cost varies with material grade, thickness (typically 1.0–3.0 mm), pattern (SWD/LWD), panel size, and finish. We do not publish unit prices: the variables interact, and a per-square-metre number quoted before the drawing is reviewed misleads more than it helps. Send the specification and we return a line-item quote for your quantities.

304 Stainless SteelPowder coat or PVDAdds a coating step for colour or extra durabilityAdded for color or extra durability
316 Stainless SteelMill / brushedHigher-alloy sheet for chloride exposureMarine / coastal

How do 304 and 316 stainless steel compare in corrosion and weathering performance for architectural use?

Material choice directly impacts long-term durability in different climates. Salt spray testing (ASTM B117) and real-world exposure data provide clear benchmarks for hotel lobbies, exterior facades, and coastal projects.

MaterialSalt Spray Hours (typical)Real Environment PerformanceBest Applications
304 Stainless Steel500–1,000 hoursGood in inland/industrial; pitting risk in salt air after 5–10 yearsInterior or protected exterior
316 Stainless Steel1,000–2,000+ hoursSuperior in coastal/marine; 20+ years with minimal maintenanceExterior facades, high-humidity, saltwater exposure
  • 304 SS offers good general corrosion resistance but is vulnerable to chlorides.
  • 316 SS contains 2–3% molybdenum, dramatically improving pitting resistance in salt-laden air.
  • Caveat: All materials perform best when properly framed to avoid water pooling; regular cleaning extends service life in all environments.
What is the open area percentage in expanded metal panels and what are the structural and visual tradeoffs for architects and specifiers?

Open area percentage (the proportion of empty space in the panel) is one of the most frequently requested specifications because it controls light transmission, ventilation, privacy, and shading while directly affecting panel stiffness.

Open Area %Typical Pattern CharacteristicsStructural ImpactVisual / Functional Tradeoffs
30–50%Smaller SWD/LWD, wider strandsHighest rigidity and load-bearing; minimal deflectionGreater opacity/privacy; reduced airflow/light
50–70%Balanced diamond patterns (most common)Good strength-to-weight; suitable for most facades/ceilingsOptimal balance of visibility, ventilation, and shade
70–85%+Larger openings, narrower strandsLower stiffness; requires denser framing or thicker gaugeMaximum light/ventilation; more transparent appearance
  • Higher open area increases airflow and daylight but reduces panel strength, often necessitating additional support frames or heavier base gauges.
  • Lower open area provides better privacy and solar shading but can restrict ventilation and increase material weight/cost.
  • Typical architectural sweet spot: 55–65% open area for most facade and ceiling applications.

What installation methods are commonly used for expanded metal panels in building facades, ceilings, and interior partitions?

Proper installation ensures structural integrity, alignment, and long-term performance. Most architects specify framed systems for ease of mounting and future maintenance.

  • Facade cladding: Panels are mounted on extruded sub-frames using Z-clips, hook-on systems, or concealed brackets; fixed with stainless steel fasteners; designed with 10–20 mm joints for thermal expansion.
  • Ceilings: Suspended on T-bar grids, lay-in framed panels, or swing-down access frames; must maintain minimum open area for sprinkler compliance; often combined with acoustic backing.
  • Interior partitions and balustrades: Framed into steel extrusions, bolted or welded to posts; tempered glass infills optional for hybrid designs.

All methods require coordination with structural engineers for wind loads (facades) and seismic requirements where applicable.

How do material grades (304 vs 316 stainless steel) influence suitability for different architectural environments?

Each grade balances weight, cost, strength, and environmental resistance. Selection is driven by exposure, budget, and maintenance expectations.

GradeWeight (kg/sqm, 2 mm thick)Relative CostKey AdvantagesLimitations
304 Stainless Steel12–16MediumHigh strength, good general corrosion resistance, modern brushed lookPitting risk in coastal or de-icing salt environments
316 Stainless Steel12–16HighestSuperior chloride resistance, longest service life outdoorsHeavier and more expensive
  • Use 304 SS for general commercial interiors and mild-climate facades.
  • Specify 316 SS for coastal hotels, marine-adjacent projects, or high-humidity atriums.
